# Trick taxonomy

An abstract map of the ways a culprit can make a table of players believe something false. It names mechanisms, not
works: there are no titles, plots or characters from any published script or novel here, on purpose. Pick one family
and one mechanism for a game; `game.json` stores them as `trick.family` (`T1`–`T8`) and `trick.mechanism`.

Every entry answers the same six questions, because these are what a party game needs from a trick:

- **False belief**: what the table believes that is not true.
- **Mechanisms**: the ways that belief gets made.
- **What it buys the culprit**: the investigative problem it solves. A trick that solves no problem for the culprit is
  decoration, and players can smell it.
- **Traces it cannot avoid**: what the world keeps after the trick. These become clues.
- **How it breaks**: the shape of the clue that defeats it.
- **Fairness at a table**: what has to be true so that players, not the host, can break it.

A party game is not a novel: every player is a suspect and a detective at once, the culprit is a player who will
hide their own clues, and the information is split across booklets. The fairness notes below are written for that.

## T1 · Time moved (时间错位)

**False belief.** The deed happened at a different time than the table thinks.

**Mechanisms.**
- T1.a *A false clock.* A timepiece, timetable or regular signal that people read the time from is wrong: set wrong,
  stopped, or running at the wrong rate.
- T1.b *A false "last seen alive".* Someone is taken for the victim after the victim is dead: a voice, a figure at a
  distance, a message sent or triggered later.
- T1.c *A delayed act.* The harm was prepared earlier and took effect later, or happened earlier while a later event
  stages it.
- T1.d *A disguised state.* The condition of the victim or the scene is changed so that any estimate of the time
  drifts. At a table, keep this to plain observations anyone can judge, never to a specialist formula.

**What it buys.** An alibi for the real moment, and a false window that points at someone else.

**Traces.** Two time sources that disagree; an object that was handled; a process (cooling, burning, melting,
dripping) that does not fit the stated time.

**How it breaks.** An independent time anchor that the culprit did not control, or a detail that someone living at the
false time could not have known.

**Fairness.** At least one independent time anchor reaches a player who has no reason to hide it. The manipulated
source is shown to be manipulable by a trace, not by the host's word.

## T2 · Place moved (地点错位)

**False belief.** It happened where the body or the evidence is; it did not.

**Mechanisms.** T2.a the victim or the object was moved after the deed; T2.b a scene was staged to look like the place
people expect; T2.c two places are confusable (same furniture, same view, same sounds) and one is taken for the other.

**What it buys.** Access and alibi arguments built on the wrong room.

**Traces.** What the true place leaves on the moved thing (dust, damp, marks, missing items); what the staged place
lacks (the ordinary mess of real use); the effort of moving (time, strength, a route someone could have seen).

**How it breaks.** A trace from the true place found on the victim or the object, or a detail of the staged place that
is wrong in a way only its regular users would notice.

**Fairness.** The ordinary state of the place is established early, in a clue or a public description, so the table
can see what is out of place. The route used for moving is visible on the floor plan players get.

## T3 · Presence faked (在场伪造)

**False belief.** Someone was in one place during the deed; they were not.

**Mechanisms.**
- T3.a *A stand-in.* Someone else is seen and taken for them.
- T3.b *A recorded or mechanical presence.* A recording, a timer or a device performs them: a voice, a light, music,
  typing, footsteps.
- T3.c *An inferred presence.* People read a sign as the person: a lamp in a window, a coat on a chair, a closed door
  with a sound behind it.
- T3.d *A short route.* A passage, a window or a shortcut makes a place everyone thinks is too far close enough.

**What it buys.** The strongest alibi there is: many witnesses who sincerely believe they sensed the culprit.

**Traces.** A performance cannot react. It misses live events, it cannot answer a question, it does not adapt to a
disturbance (a noise, a change in power, an interruption). A device must be prepared and put away; a stand-in differs
in body, habit or knowledge; a short route leaves wear, dirt or a door someone should have heard.

**How it breaks.** Something happens during the performance that a present person would have reacted to, and the
performance does not; or the preparation and disposal of the device surface.

**Fairness.** The means is public knowledge in the world (the table knows the device exists and can be used this way)
before anyone needs it. The observation that the performance failed to react sits with at least one innocent player,
worded as something they noticed but did not understand. Someone at the table can supply the principle that makes the
observation meaningful, in their own trade's words.

## T4 · Identity swapped (身份错置)

**False belief.** A person is who they seem to be.

**Mechanisms.** T4.a the victim is not who the table thinks; T4.b the culprit lives under another name or role;
T4.c two people are taken for one, or one for two; T4.d a person believed dead or absent is present, or the reverse.

**What it buys.** Motive hidden behind the wrong biography; presence or absence the table never questions.

**Traces.** Knowledge the person should have and lacks (or has and should not); body details, handwriting, habits,
language; paperwork that does not agree.

**How it breaks.** A test of knowledge or habit that only the real person would pass, set up by the world rather than
by the host.

**Fairness.** The possibility of the swap is planted early with a reason that has nothing to do with the crime.
Swaps between look-alikes need their likeness shown before the deed, not revealed at the end.

## T5 · Hand hidden (真凶另手)

**False belief.** The person present did it, or nobody did it on purpose.

**Mechanisms.** T5.a a trap or a substance placed earlier by someone absent; T5.b the victim's own habit or action
made to do the work; T5.c another person steered into acting without knowing the purpose; T5.d two separate acts,
where the harmless one is blamed for the harmful one.

**What it buys.** The culprit is elsewhere at the moment of harm, and the person present takes the blame.

**Traces.** The setup (purchase, access, preparation); the knowledge of the victim's habits that only some had; a
message or instruction that steered the unwitting hand.

**How it breaks.** Evidence of the preparation tied to the culprit, and proof that the person present could not have
known what they were doing.

**Fairness.** The victim's habit, or the steered person's instruction, reaches the table as a clue. The unwitting hand
is given enough in their own booklet to discover that they were used.

## T6 · Way in or out (出入之谜)

**False belief.** Nobody could have entered or left the place.

**Mechanisms.** T6.a the closure was made after the deed, from outside; T6.b the place was not closed at the time
people assume; T6.c the watch on the entrance had a gap; T6.d there is another way in that the table does not know
or has not thought of.

**What it buys.** Suicide, accident or an impossible crime instead of a suspect.

**Traces.** The tool or the movement that closed it; the moment the watch lapsed; wear on the other way.

**How it breaks.** A detail of the closure that could only have been made from the wrong side or at the wrong time.

**Fairness.** At most one hidden way, shown on the plan players get (not revealed at the end). The watcher's gap is
held by a player, preferably the watcher, who has a reason to hide it that is unrelated to the crime.

## T7 · Trail planted (伪造指向)

**False belief.** The evidence points at the person who did it.

**Mechanisms.** T7.a an object planted; T7.b a writing forged; T7.c a struggle or an accident staged; T7.d a false
statement made, or a true one arranged to be misread.

**What it buys.** A scapegoat, and an investigation that stops early.

**Traces.** The planted thing is too convenient or wrong in a detail (placed after the fact, handled by the wrong hand,
from the wrong time); the forger's knowledge shows through.

**How it breaks.** A detail showing the evidence arrived after the deed, or could not have come from the person it
names.

**Fairness.** The planted evidence has a checkable history (when the object was last seen, who could reach it). The
framed player gets material to defend themselves with, so being framed is something to do, not something to endure.

## T8 · Wrong case (案件错位)

**False belief.** The table knows which crime it is investigating.

**Mechanisms.** T8.a the intended victim was someone else; T8.b the crime is cover for another act (a theft, an
escape, a second death); T8.c the motive everyone chases belongs to a different event; T8.d there are two crimes by
two people, one hiding behind the other.

**What it buys.** Every alibi and motive the table tests belongs to the wrong question; suspects are cleared of the case
they are asked about.

**Traces.** What the cover was meant to hide is still missing or out of place; the true target's situation.

**How it breaks.** A clue that only makes sense if the question is different.

**Fairness.** The true question is askable from the table's material, not only from the reveal. When a detective proves
someone innocent of the case and the facts still point along the culprit's path, the family is working; when nothing
points there, it is unfair.

## Fairness conditions for any family

These are the conditions `chain_check.py` tests where structure can show them (the code is in brackets); the rest are
for the blind detective and for people.

1. Every step of the solution stands on clues that a player receives: in a booklet, on a search card, or from the host
   [J02]. Nothing only the host knows.
2. No clue the culprit alone holds is load-bearing: the culprit will keep it [J08].
3. The culprit's cover breaks through the clues written for that, and those clues carry the break in their words; the
   same words appear nowhere else a player reads [J05].
4. Elimination is not enough: at least one innocent stays a live suspect until the positive proof, and there is a
   positive proof [J07].
5. Every red herring can be resolved by something players get; no thread is left hanging [J04].
6. No player page, card or announcement states the answer [J06, J13].
7. Every role has a goal, a secret, something to tell, and a stake in the chain [J09]; no role has far less to do than
   the busiest [J10].
8. The key steps have two routes. If one player forgets or refuses to share, the game still ends [J11 warns].
9. The clue that raises a suspicion and the clue that proves it are different clues. Suspicion without proof is where
   a table guesses; the detective's grade separates the two (RIGHT NAME, COVER NOT BROKEN).

## Choosing a trick for a premise

- Choose after the premise and the cast, not before: the trick must solve a problem this culprit has, in this place.
- Write the break clue first, then hand it to an innocent who noticed it without understanding it. Give the principle
  that makes it meaningful to a different player, in their own words.
- Decide which clues raise the hypothesis and which prove it. The proof should come from something the culprit could
  not control.
- Ask what the laziest way to satisfy the trick would be. If a simpler plan would have worked for the culprit, the
  table will ask why they did not use it; answer that inside the story.
